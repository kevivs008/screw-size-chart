Pilot Holes for Hardwood Screws: Size, Depth and Method

A pilot hole is a guide hole drilled before the screw goes in. In hardwood it is sized close to the screw’s core, so the threads still cut fresh fibre but the wood does not split. For a #8 wood screw, a typical starting point is 1/8 in in hardwood (7/64 in in softwood), plus an 11/64 in clearance hole in the top piece when you are joining two boards. Treat every figure as a starting point and test it in an offcut of the same board, because species, moisture and the screw’s make all shift the answer. The full list of sizes is in the wood screw size chart.

Key takeaways

  • Two holes do two jobs: a pilot hole where the threads bite, and a clearance hole where the shank passes freely.
  • Size the pilot by eye: the drill bit should hide the screw’s solid core but leave the thread visible.
  • Published sizes are typical starting points, not rules. Test in scrap.
  • Never use a machine-screw tap drill as a wood pilot hole. The families have separate tables.

Why hardwood needs a pilot hole

A screw acts as a wedge. In softwood the fibres compress and make room; in dense hardwood they resist, and the pressure goes sideways instead. The result is a split board, a screw that wanders off its mark, or a head that twists off before the screw seats. A pilot hole removes the wood that would otherwise be forced aside, so the threads only have to cut the fibre around it. Splits are most likely near ends and edges, where there is less wood to resist the wedge.

Two holes, two jobs

When you join two pieces, the screw passes through one and threads into the other, and each needs its own hole.

  • Clearance (shank) hole in the top piece: about the diameter of the screw, so the shank slides through without gripping. This lets the threads pull the lower piece up tight against the upper one. If the threads also grip the top piece, they can hold the boards apart.
  • Pilot hole in the lower piece: smaller than the clearance hole, so the threads have wood to cut.
  • Countersink for flat heads: a shallow cone so the head sits flush. Cut it after the clearance hole, in the top surface.

Size the pilot with the bit test

Hold the drill bit up in front of the screw, and look from the side. In hardwood, the bit should cover the screw’s solid core, the narrow body between the threads, but leave the threads themselves sticking out on both sides. A bit that is narrower than the core makes the screw hard to drive; a bit that reaches the thread tips leaves the threads nothing to cut. If you also hold a screw in the hole after drilling, it should turn in with firm hand pressure on the driver and not spin freely.

Depth and method

  1. Mark the screw position and clamp the pieces so they cannot shift.
  2. Drill the clearance hole through the top piece, then add the countersink if the head is flat.
  3. With the pieces clamped, drill the pilot hole into the lower piece through the clearance hole, so the two holes line up.
  4. Drill the pilot to about the depth the threads will travel. Wrap a piece of tape on the bit or use a stop collar to mark it.
  5. Rub the threads on wax or soap, which makes the screw easier to drive in dense wood, and drive it with a steady turn until the head is seated. Stop there; driving further can strip the hole or snap the head.

Use the screw as the judge. If it binds before it seats, a slightly deeper or slightly wider pilot usually fixes it. If it spins freely, the pilot is too large, and the next screw needs a smaller bit.

Near ends, edges and each other

Stay away from ends and edges where the job allows it, since the wedge effect is strongest there. Stagger screws so they do not line up along one grain line, because a row of wedges in the same grain is a common way to split a board. Both habits matter more in hardwood than in softwood.

Don’t borrow a machine-screw hole size

The size numbers overlap between machine screws and wood screws, but the holes do not. A #8 machine screw uses a #29 tap drill, which is 0.136 in, to cut its thread in metal. A hardwood pilot for a #8 wood screw is typically 1/8 in, or 0.125 in. A #29 hole in wood would leave the threads almost nothing to cut. Each family has its own table; the drill-bit finder picks the right one from the screw you choose.
