Choosing the right chart

Start with how the screw is labelled. A letter M followed by a number means metric. A # sign, or a fraction such as 1/4, means an inch size. If the screw has a sharp tapered point and a deep thread, it is almost certainly a wood screw. Each family is kept apart because the same number means different things in each: #8 is 0.164 in for both machine and wood screws, but the thread, the tap drill and the clearance hole all differ.

If you only have the screw in your hand, measure the outside of the thread with a caliper and use Match my screw on the drill bit page. Measure the thread crests, not the head, and expect a small error.

How to read a machine screw size

A size such as #8-32 UNC has three parts: the gauge number (8), the threads per inch (32) and the thread series (UNC, unified coarse). Sizes up to #12 use gauge numbers; from 1/4 inch up they are written as fractions, for example 1/4-20. Each gauge number maps to a diameter by 0.060 + 0.013 x number, so #8 is 0.164 in.

UNC or UNF?

Coarse threads (UNC) are the default: they assemble faster and are less likely to cross-thread. Fine threads (UNF) give finer adjustment and are used where the wall is thin or the load is better spread over more threads. For the same diameter the two are not interchangeable, so match the thread count printed on the part: 1/4-20 is coarse with a #7 tap drill, 1/4-28 is fine with a #3.

Tap drill or clearance hole?

Drill the tap drill size in the part you will thread. Drill the clearance hole in the part the screw passes through. Close, normal and loose fits trade alignment against how forgiving the holes are to position. A common mistake is using the clearance drill in the part you meant to tap, which leaves nothing for the thread to cut.

Gauge numbers and diameter

Wood screw gauges use the same arithmetic as numbered machine screws: diameter in inches is 0.060 + 0.013 x gauge, so #8 is 0.164 in. The head type and length are separate from the gauge.

Pilot hole or clearance hole?

The pilot hole goes into the board the screw bites into. It should be about the diameter of the shank under the thread, so the thread has wood to cut and the wood does not split. In hardwood it is larger than in softwood: for a #8 screw the chart gives 7/64 in softwood and 1/8 in hardwood. The clearance hole goes into the top piece, where the screw should slide through freely so the joint pulls tight.

Testing the hole

Hardness varies between species and even between boards, which is why published charts disagree. Drill a pilot in an offcut first. If the screw is hard to drive or the wood splits, go up one bit size; if the screw spins without gripping, go down one.

Machine Screw vs Wood Screw: What’s the Difference?

A machine screw has a straight shank and a standardised thread made to mate with a nut or a tapped hole. A wood screw has a tapered, pointed body and a coarse thread that cuts its own path through wood fibres, usually with a smooth shank under the head. The size numbers overlap, which is the trap: a #8 of either type is 0.164 in across, but the holes they need are different. Each family has its own chart, so use the machine screw size chart or the wood screw size chart for the screw in your hand, never one for the other.

Key takeaways

  • A machine screw needs an internal thread to meet: a nut or a tapped hole. A wood screw makes its own thread in the wood.
  • Look for the tell: a smooth shank under the head and a sharp point mean wood; a thread running the full length with a blunt end means machine.
  • The same size number takes a different hole: a #8 machine screw is tapped with a #29 drill, while a #8 wood screw gets a pilot hole sized to the wood.
  • Never borrow a hole size from the other family’s table.

How the threads differ

A machine screw has a parallel (straight) thread of constant diameter, finished with a blunt or chamfered end. The thread belongs to a standard series: Unified inch threads (UNC and UNF, ASME B1.1) or ISO metric threads. A standard series matters because the screw is meant to mate with a thread cut or moulded to the same standard, so a #8-32 machine screw fits any #8-32 nut or tapped hole.

A wood screw has no mating thread to match. Its thread is deep and widely spaced, and the body typically tapers toward a sharp point so the screw can start in wood and pull itself in. Wood screws are covered by ASME B18.6.1, which sets the thread length for cut-thread screws at roughly two thirds of the nominal length. Below that, the shank is smooth.

One wrinkle: not every wood screw tapers. Traditional wood screws have a shank that matches the outside diameter of the thread, and they pair with a tapered drill bit. Many modern production screws have a straight shank slightly smaller than the thread’s outer diameter, so a straight bit does the job. Both are wood screws; the second type is just more forgiving about the drill.

How each one holds

A machine screw holds through thread engagement with its partner. In a nut or a tapped hole, the screw thread and the internal thread mesh, and tightening clamps the parts together.

A wood screw holds by the thread cutting into the wood fibres of the lower piece. The smooth shank is not decoration. When joining two boards, the shank passes through a clearance hole in the top board, so the threads engage only the lower board and tightening draws the two together. If the threads also grip the top board, they can hold the boards apart.

Where each is used

Machine screws go where there is a thread to meet: tapped holes in metal, nuts, appliance panels, electronics, equipment housings. Wood screws go into wood and wood-based boards such as plywood and particleboard. Put a machine screw into plain wood and there is nothing for it to engage; put a wood screw into a tapped hole and its thread form does not match. They are not interchangeable.

A third family, sheet-metal or self-tapping screws, sits between the two and is outside this guide. The same rule applies: use the table for the family you actually have.

Why the same size number takes a different hole

The numbering is the source of the confusion. A #8 machine screw has a nominal major diameter of 0.164 in (4.166 mm), and the nominal diameter listed for a #8 wood screw is also 0.164 in. The number says how big the screw is. It says nothing about the hole, which depends on the job the hole does.

Don’t cross the streams. A #29 hole is 0.136 in, far larger than a typical 1/8 in (0.125 in) hardwood pilot, and it would leave a wood screw’s threads very little wood to cut.

How to tell them apart in your hand

  • Shank: a smooth, unthreaded section under the head points to a wood screw.
  • Tip: a sharp point suggests wood; a blunt or flat end suggests machine.
  • Thread spacing: wide and coarse suggests wood; fine and even suggests machine.
  • Test fit: a machine screw turns freely into a matching nut, and a wood screw won’t.
  • Calipers: read the thread crests, and expect the smooth shank of a wood screw to measure slightly over nominal.

Which one do you need?

Let the material decide. If the screw is going into a thread (a nut, a tapped hole in metal), you need a machine screw of the matching size and thread series. If it is going into wood or a wood-based board, you need a wood screw. For any load-bearing or safety-related fixing, follow the designer’s or manufacturer’s specification rather than a general guide. The screw size chart hub links to all three families side by side.

UNC vs UNF Threads: What’s the Difference?

UNC (coarse) and UNF (fine) are two thread series for the same screw diameter. Both are defined in ASME B1.1, and both use the same 60° thread form; they differ only in how many threads fit into an inch. A 1/4 in screw is 1/4-20 in the coarse series and 1/4-28 in the fine series. Coarse threads are fewer and deeper; fine threads are more numerous and shallower. They are not interchangeable, and the tap drill changes with the series. If you have the screw in your hand, count the threads per inch, then look up the size in the machine screw size chart.

Key takeaways

  • UNC and UNF share a diameter and differ in threads per inch (TPI): 1/4-20 against 1/4-28, #8-32 against #8-36, #10-24 against #10-32.
  • A UNC screw will not run into a UNF nut, or the reverse. Counting TPI is the only reliable test.
  • The tap drill follows the thread: the finer series usually calls for a slightly larger drill.
  • Whether fine or coarse suits a job is an engineering decision. Follow the drawing or specification rather than a rule of thumb.

What UNC and UNF actually mean

The letters stand for Unified National Coarse and Unified National Fine, the names of the two main series in the Unified inch system. Each diameter gets a standard TPI in each series, so “1/4-20” and “1/4-28” are complete names: the diameter, then the TPI. Many callouts add the series and a fit class, as in 1/4-20 UNC-2A for an external thread. The 2A (external) or 2B (internal) part is the fit class, which sets how tightly the parts mate. A few of the very smallest sizes exist in only one series; #0, for example, is made as 0-80 only.

Two things stand out. First, the gap in TPI is small for the small sizes: #8-32 and #8-36 differ by only four threads per inch, which you cannot see by eye. A pitch gauge or a careful count is the only way to tell them apart. Second, the tap drills are not the same, except at #8.

Why the tap drill changes with the series

A tap drill leaves enough material for the tap to cut a thread of the right depth, and a finer thread is shallower. So the hole for the finer series sits closer to the screw’s full diameter. A standard shortcut gives about 75% thread depth: tap drill ≈ major diameter − 0.975 ÷ TPI. For 1/4-20 that is 0.2500 − 0.975 ÷ 20 = 0.2013 in, which points to a #7 (0.201 in). For 1/4-28 it is 0.2500 − 0.975 ÷ 28 = 0.2152 in, close to a #3 (0.213 in). At #8 the two series happen to land on the same #29 (0.136 in), so one drill serves both.

When a fine thread is chosen

Designers pick the fine series for reasons that come down to geometry. Each turn advances the screw by one pitch, which is 0.0500 in at 20 TPI and 0.0357 in at 28 TPI, so a fine thread gives finer adjustment. In a short or thin section, a fine thread fits more turns into the same depth: a 1/4 in deep engagement holds 5 turns of a 20 TPI thread and 7 turns of a 28 TPI thread. And often the reason is simply that the drawing says UNF.

You will also read that fine threads are stronger or resist vibration better, and that coarse threads are tougher. Whether any of that matters depends on the material, the joint and the specification, so this guide does not rank them. For anything load-bearing or safety-related, follow the specification or ask a qualified engineer.

When coarse is the default

Coarse is the general-purpose series. It is the one most hardware is made in, it starts faster by hand, and it tolerates a nick or a little dirt in the thread better than a shallow fine thread does. If a drawing does not say otherwise and a part came with coarse screws, coarse is the likely series for replacements, once a count confirms it.

How to tell which one you have

  1. Measure the major diameter with calipers and find the size (a numbered size, or a fraction).
  2. Count the threads per inch, or match a pitch gauge leaf with no light between the teeth.
  3. Compare the count with the table above or the full chart.

Test a nut by hand. If it spins on for a turn or two and then binds, stop; it is the wrong series, and forcing it will damage the threads on both parts. Metric threads follow the same idea: the coarse pitch is the default (M8 x 1.25) and fine pitches are marked in the callout (M8 x 1).
