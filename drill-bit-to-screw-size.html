How the four drill series work

Drill bits are sold in four overlapping sets. Number drills run from #1 (0.228 in) down to #80. Letter drills run from A (0.234 in) up to Z. Fractional drills step in sixty-fourths of an inch from 1/64 in. Metric drills are sized in millimetres. The finder shows the nearest bit in each, with the difference from the ideal size, so you can use whichever set you own.

Choosing the purpose

Pick tap drill when you will cut a thread with a tap. Pick clearance when the screw should pass through freely. Pick pilot for wood screws. If the nearest bit is larger than the ideal size, expect a looser fit; if it is smaller, expect to work harder to drive or tap. When in doubt, test on scrap material.

Pilot Holes for Hardwood Screws: Size, Depth and Method

A pilot hole is a guide hole drilled before the screw goes in. In hardwood it is sized close to the screw’s core, so the threads still cut fresh fibre but the wood does not split. For a #8 wood screw, a typical starting point is 1/8 in in hardwood (7/64 in in softwood), plus an 11/64 in clearance hole in the top piece when you are joining two boards. Treat every figure as a starting point and test it in an offcut of the same board, because species, moisture and the screw’s make all shift the answer. The full list of sizes is in the wood screw size chart.

Key takeaways

  • Two holes do two jobs: a pilot hole where the threads bite, and a clearance hole where the shank passes freely.
  • Size the pilot by eye: the drill bit should hide the screw’s solid core but leave the thread visible.
  • Published sizes are typical starting points, not rules. Test in scrap.
  • Never use a machine-screw tap drill as a wood pilot hole. The families have separate tables.

Why hardwood needs a pilot hole

A screw acts as a wedge. In softwood the fibres compress and make room; in dense hardwood they resist, and the pressure goes sideways instead. The result is a split board, a screw that wanders off its mark, or a head that twists off before the screw seats. A pilot hole removes the wood that would otherwise be forced aside, so the threads only have to cut the fibre around it. Splits are most likely near ends and edges, where there is less wood to resist the wedge.

Two holes, two jobs

When you join two pieces, the screw passes through one and threads into the other, and each needs its own hole.

  • Clearance (shank) hole in the top piece: about the diameter of the screw, so the shank slides through without gripping. This lets the threads pull the lower piece up tight against the upper one. If the threads also grip the top piece, they can hold the boards apart.
  • Pilot hole in the lower piece: smaller than the clearance hole, so the threads have wood to cut.
  • Countersink for flat heads: a shallow cone so the head sits flush. Cut it after the clearance hole, in the top surface.

Size the pilot with the bit test

Hold the drill bit up in front of the screw, and look from the side. In hardwood, the bit should cover the screw’s solid core, the narrow body between the threads, but leave the threads themselves sticking out on both sides. A bit that is narrower than the core makes the screw hard to drive; a bit that reaches the thread tips leaves the threads nothing to cut. If you also hold a screw in the hole after drilling, it should turn in with firm hand pressure on the driver and not spin freely.

Depth and method

  1. Mark the screw position and clamp the pieces so they cannot shift.
  2. Drill the clearance hole through the top piece, then add the countersink if the head is flat.
  3. With the pieces clamped, drill the pilot hole into the lower piece through the clearance hole, so the two holes line up.
  4. Drill the pilot to about the depth the threads will travel. Wrap a piece of tape on the bit or use a stop collar to mark it.
  5. Rub the threads on wax or soap, which makes the screw easier to drive in dense wood, and drive it with a steady turn until the head is seated. Stop there; driving further can strip the hole or snap the head.

Use the screw as the judge. If it binds before it seats, a slightly deeper or slightly wider pilot usually fixes it. If it spins freely, the pilot is too large, and the next screw needs a smaller bit.

Near ends, edges and each other

Stay away from ends and edges where the job allows it, since the wedge effect is strongest there. Stagger screws so they do not line up along one grain line, because a row of wedges in the same grain is a common way to split a board. Both habits matter more in hardwood than in softwood.

Don’t borrow a machine-screw hole size

The size numbers overlap between machine screws and wood screws, but the holes do not. A #8 machine screw uses a #29 tap drill, which is 0.136 in, to cut its thread in metal. A hardwood pilot for a #8 wood screw is typically 1/8 in, or 0.125 in. A #29 hole in wood would leave the threads almost nothing to cut. Each family has its own table; the drill-bit finder picks the right one from the screw you choose.

Tap Drill Sizes Explained: How to Choose the Right Drill

A tap drill is the drill that makes the hole before a tap cuts the thread. It is smaller than the screw, because the tap needs wall material to cut a thread into. The common charts pick the drill that leaves about 75% of full thread depth: a #8-32 screw uses a #29 drill (0.136 in), a 1/4-20 uses a #7, and an M8 x 1.25 uses 6.80 mm. Do not confuse the tap drill with the clearance hole, which is the larger hole the screw passes through. To get the right drill for any size, use the drill-bit finder, or read the chart for inch machine screws or metric screws.

Key takeaways

  • The tap drill makes the hole that will be threaded. The clearance hole is for the part the screw passes through. They are different holes with different sizes.
  • Inch shortcut: tap drill ≈ major diameter − 0.975 ÷ TPI. Metric shortcut: tap drill ≈ major diameter − pitch.
  • A finer thread is shallower, so it needs a larger tap drill.
  • If the tap’s maker prints a recommended drill, use it; charts can differ by a drill size.

What a tap drill does

A tap does not make a hole; it cuts a thread into the wall of one that already exists. That hole must be smaller than the screw’s major diameter. If it were as wide as the screw, the tap would find nothing to cut. If it were much narrower, the tap would have to remove too much material, and tapping becomes hard work. The usual compromise is a hole that leaves roughly 75% of the full thread depth, which is what standard tap drill charts are built on.

The finer series always gets the larger drill at the same diameter: #21 against #25 on a #10, #3 against #7 on a 1/4 in, 7.00 mm against 6.80 mm on an M8. A finer thread is shallower, so the hole can sit closer to the full diameter.

A shortcut you can check in your head

You can estimate the drill without a chart. In inch sizes, take the major diameter and subtract 0.975 divided by the threads per inch. In metric sizes, subtract the pitch from the major diameter. The 0.975 comes from taking 75% of the thread height on each side of the hole, the convention behind the common charts.

  • #8-32: 0.1640 − 0.975 ÷ 32 = 0.1335 in. The chart picks the #29 (0.136 in), a nearby standard drill.
  • #10-24: 0.1900 − 0.975 ÷ 24 = 0.1494 in, which is the #25 (0.1495 in).
  • 1/4-20: 0.2500 − 0.975 ÷ 20 = 0.2013 in, which is the #7 (0.201 in).
  • M8 x 1.25: 8 − 1.25 = 6.75 mm, so the standard 6.8 mm drill.
  • M6 x 0.75: 6 − 0.75 = 5.25 mm, a drill size in its own right.

Round to the nearest drill the chart offers. The shortcut shows you whether a chart value is sensible, but for a real job, read the chart.

Tap drill vs clearance hole

One screw needs two different holes when it joins two parts. Take a #8-32 machine screw: the part that holds the thread gets the #29 tap drill (0.136 in) and is then tapped. The part the screw passes through gets a clearance hole, which for a close fit is a #18. The tap drill is deliberately smaller than the screw; the clearance hole is deliberately larger. Using one for the other either leaves no thread or leaves the screw rattling.

Practical notes

  • Substitutes: a 3.5 mm drill (0.1378 in) is close to a #29 (0.136 in). It is a little larger, so it leaves slightly less thread than the chart intends.
  • Charts can differ: two published charts sometimes list drills one size apart for the same thread, because they choose slightly different engagement. If the tap’s maker prints a recommended drill, use it.
  • Blind holes: drill deeper than the thread length you need so the tap and its chips have room.
  • Cutting oil: lubricate when tapping metal, and back the tap out regularly to break the chip.
  • Wood is different: a pilot hole for a wood screw follows a separate table. Never use a tap drill as a wood pilot.
